I had a bad habit of treating "breakfast tea" as a category rather than a description. Something brisk, something that could stand up to milk, something you drank because you needed the caffeine and not because you were paying attention. Assam-based blends mostly, reliable, a little blunt.

Then a friend brought back a Yunnan Gold — a Dian Hong, golden-tipped, from a small producer near Fengqing — and steeped it for me the way it's supposed to be steeped: no milk, water just off the boil, short first infusion. What came out of the pot had almost nothing in common with what I'd been calling breakfast tea for a decade.

## What was actually in the cup

The first note was cocoa, unexpectedly, followed by something closer to sweet potato than the malty-brisk profile I was braced for. No astringency to speak of, which was the real surprise — I'd assumed the bite was the point, that a tea strong enough to wake you up had to also rough you up a little on the way down. This one didn't. It was fuller than that, warmer, and it held its sweetness through a second and third steep instead of collapsing into bitterness the way lower-grade black teas tend to once you push past the first infusion.

## Reading the leaf, not just the name

The lesson wasn't really about Yunnan Gold specifically. It was that "black tea" as a category hides enormous variance, and the golden tips — the actual young buds, not just a marketing name — tell you something real about how the leaf was picked and processed. A tea heavy with golden tips is a tea somebody made a decision to slow down for, and that decision shows up in the cup whether or not you know to look for it.

I still keep an everyday breakfast blend around for mornings that don't have room for a proper gongfu session. But I no longer think of "brisk and unsubtle" as breakfast tea's ceiling. It's just the low end of a much wider range, and I'd been drinking from the bottom of it on purpose.
