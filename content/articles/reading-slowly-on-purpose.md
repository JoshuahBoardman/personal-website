I used to track how many books I read in a year. The number went up steadily for a while and then I noticed I couldn't tell you much about most of them past a one-line summary. I'd read them the way you'd clear a queue.

Somewhere in there I started deliberately slowing down — not for difficult books, where slowness is forced on you anyway, but for ordinary ones. A chapter a night instead of three. Rereading a paragraph that landed well instead of carrying the momentum into the next one. It felt unproductive at first, almost embarrassing, like admitting I couldn't keep pace with my own reading list.

## What the slowness actually bought

What changed wasn't comprehension exactly — I wasn't misunderstanding books at the faster pace. It was retention of texture rather than plot. Plot survives a fast read fine; you remember what happened. What doesn't survive is the specific shape of a sentence, the small formal choice an author made that isn't load-bearing to the story but is the reason the book felt like *that* book and not another one telling the same events.

## The number stopped mattering

I don't track a books-per-year count anymore. Partly because the number was measuring throughput, and throughput was never actually the thing I wanted from reading — if it were, I'd read summaries. Partly because the count created a quiet pressure to choose shorter, easier books to keep the number healthy, which is exactly backward from what I actually want to be reading.

This one's still a bit of a working theory more than a finished thought — I'm not fully sure yet whether the slower pace is doing what I think it's doing, or whether I've just gotten better at noticing things I was always retaining. Worth revisiting once I've got more than a few months of the habit behind me.
